Grid ceiling: does a fixed headroom beat the multiple?

The top of the wealth axis is top(v) = max(hiYears 60, (v/spend) * headroom 6), so it stretches with
opening wealth. Conjecture: because the axis is log-spaced with a fixed point count, a wealthy
household gets a coarser cliff than a lean one, and a fixed ceiling would put more points where the
cliff is.

The arithmetic first, on the 41 band households (headroom-diag): 35 of 41 have a ceiling above 60
years; the worst stretch is 33.3 years of opening wealth giving a 200-year ceiling. But log spacing
means the cost is small: neighbouring points go from 18.3% apart at a 60-year ceiling to 22.1% at
200, and the count of points across 5 to 40 years of spending goes from 12 to 10. Mean over the 41:
11.22 points against 12.00. So the resolution on offer is about one point in twelve.

Measured on the six most-stretched households (bias.mjs, 40 points, 1,500 held-out paths), table
bias in survival points, table minus the simulated truth of the table's own policy:

  household   multiple (now)   fixed 60   fixed 40
  S054            -0.4           -0.2       -1.6
  S234            -0.6           -1.3       -5.6
  S378            -0.5           -0.5       -3.0
  S390            -0.6           -0.8       -2.9
  S414            -2.7           -3.6      -12.8
  S126          -89.3          -89.2      -90.9   (see the anomaly below)

Verdict: the multiple stays. A fixed 60-year ceiling is a wash at best, better on one household and
worse on three; a fixed 40-year ceiling is much worse everywhere. The mechanism is the one the
headroom was put there for: wealth that compounds past the top of the axis is clamped to the top
point, so the table reads low, and that pessimism is larger than the resolution the tighter ceiling
buys. Cutting the ceiling trades a first-order error for a second-order gain.

Two things the run showed that the question did not ask about.

The knob could not be set. `opts.headroom || 6` read a headroom of 0 as absent, so the first pass of
this test ran three identical grids and printed identical numbers to four decimals. Changed to `??`.
No production path passes 0, so nothing else moves.

Decisions did not change. The best move at the opening position is the same index under all three
ceilings on all six households, and the simulated survival of the policy moves by at most 0.3 points.
The ceiling is a forecasting parameter here, not a tactical one, which is the same thing the shift
mixture turned out to be.

ANOMALY, not chased: S126 (early-bridge, pension-heavy, comfortable) reads 7.5% at the opening cell
while its own policy simulates at 96.8%, an 89-point gap that barely moves with the ceiling. Every
other household is inside 3 points. The published results are unaffected because the experiment
scores by simulation rather than by the opening cell, but either bias.mjs is stale in a second way or
the table's t=0 read is wrong for bridge households. Worth its own diagnosis.
